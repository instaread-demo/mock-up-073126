Northern Colorado’s Average First Snow Is Closer Than You Think

This summer in Northern Colorado has been a scorcher. This past weekend, Denver International Airport broke a record for the hottest day on July 26, 2026, beating out the previous record in that location from 1910, with a high of 103.

With 103-degree days, many of us are longing for cooler weather, and some may even be begging for snow to be falling from the sky. You may feel like summer will never end in Colorado, but we're not far off from our first average snowfall in Northern Colorado.

When Northern Colorado Usually Sees Its First Snow

Winter in Northern Colorado this past season was more or less nonexistent. The lack of snow was nice, for one reason, and one reason only. I didn't have to shovel my driveway. However, many industries suffered from the lack of snow. The ski industry and agriculture are just two big ones that come to mind.

This winter, I am hoping we get a decent amount of snow, because lord knows, we need it. Bad. According to the Colorado State University Colorado Climate Center, the average first snowfall of the season isn't too far away.

READ MORE: Colorado Wildfires Burn Thousands of Acres Across the State

The first average snowfall usually happens on the Front Range around the last week of October. Looking at the calendar, that puts us at hopefully getting our first dose of winter around October 25.

With the average first snowfall date around the last week of October, we're looking at less than three months until the snowflakes start flying in the sky. It's kind of hard to imagine it right now with 90-degree temperatures, but winter is right around the corner.

Colorado Could Use a Snowier Winter

As much as I want winter and snow to arrive in Northern Colorado, I am hopeful that we get a great fall this year. Even though the lack of rain has given us our struggles, there is still nothing quite like a crisp, fall Colorado day.

These Creepy Water Creatures are Found in Colorado

Gallery Credit: Kelsey Nistel

Missouri Department of Conservation

Mud Puppy

Despite what their name might suggest, mudpuppies are anything but cute. These slimy salamanders are native to Colorado's Western Slope and are commonly seen at Highline Lake. They are permanently aquatic amphibians that are typically about 8 to 13 inches long but have the potential to grow to 19 inches.

EPKIN/Thinkstock

Catfish

Although they are totally harmless, Colorado's channel catfish have some of the freakiest facial features, including long barbels (whiskers). Channel cats are native to eastern Colorado and have been stocked in warmer rivers and reservoirs throughout the state. Catfish larger than 30 pounds have been caught in Colorado - can you imagine running into one of those big guys while going for a dip?!

Getty Images/iStockphoto

Tiger Muskie

While far less vicious than their mammal namesake, the teeth of tiger muskies are still something you don't want to mess with! These fish are notoriously difficult for anglers to reel in due to their size, strength, and erratic eating patterns. In Colorado, tiger muskies have been recorded to reach over 40 pounds.

Getty Images

Bullfrog Tadpole

Okay - bullfrog tadpoles toe the line between being cute and creepy. But feeling one swim over your foot in a lake or pond might make you squirm.

Getty Images/iStockphoto

Northern Pike

Northern pike in Colorado can grow well over 40 inches and push upwards to 30 pounds. Some anglers aim to catch these big fish, but coming face-to-face with one underwater would be pretty frightening.

Chris A. Taylor, Prairie Research Institute​/ CPW

Devil Crayfish

Several different crayfish species exist in Colorado, but devil crayfish are arguably the most ominous. They may be little, but those claws can still pinch. The species is native to the South Platte drainage in northeast Colorado.

Photo by Ian Gardiner/CPW

Creeping ancylid

Creeping ancylids cling to rocks, cobbles, mussel shells, and larger aquatic plants in rivers, streams, and lakes. It's very rare to come across this type of mollusk in Colorado, but they can be found in the Arkansas, Colorado, Platte, Rio Grande, and Yampa River drainages.
