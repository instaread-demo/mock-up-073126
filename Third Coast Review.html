On the Road: Music@Menlo Chamber Music Festival and Institute Entertains and Teaches, Sometimes All at Once

  • Louis Harris
  • July 27, 2026
  • Classical, Festivals, Music

The 24th season of the Music@Menlo Chamber Music Festival and Institute continued this week outside of Palo Alto, Calif. Spread over three weeks in July and August, Music@Menlo synthesizes teaching and performance of classical chamber music into a compelling experience for students and audience members alike. This year’s Music@Menlo is entitled Redux.

Pianist Wu Han and cellist David Finckel founded the festival in 2002 and have been the artistic directors ever since. Next year’s 25th season will have new artistic directors, Dmitri Atapine and Hyeyeon Park, who will head the festival in 2027.

Music@Menlo opens up to the public the process of music teaching and coaching. The setting is Menlo School, a college preparatory middle and high school in Allerton, Calif., which has the spaces to accommodate performances, rehearsals, and coaching.

The institute hosts two sets of students. The first is the International Program of 11 young professionals, aged 20-30. Ensembles made up of these students perform in Prelude Concerts in the late afternoon. The other group is the Smith Family Young Performers Program for 22 younger students, aged 13-19, ensembles of whom perform Koret Young Performers Concerts.

During the course of a day, students interact with each other and hone their skills and enhance their musical studies with professional coaches and mentors. Faculty include some big names in music performance and education. In addition to Han and Finckel, faculty members include Benjamin Beilman, Sir Stephen Hough, Paul Neubauer, Gilles Vonsattel, Gilbert Kalisch, Jean-Efflam Bavouzet, and several others. The public is invited to attend all activities. 

On most days there’s a session where a well-known professional critiques a performance of chamber music by both sets of students. It was fascinating to observe pianist Gilbert Kalisch coach students through the set of variations in Ludwig van Beethoven’s Gassenhauer Trio in B-Flat Major, Op. 11, using a violin instead of a clarinet. He then coached a piano quartet through the finale of Robert Schumann’s Piano Quartet in E-Flat Major, Op. 47. The results were evident when this same quartet performed the whole work the next day at a Prelude Concert. 

On the following day, violinist Chad Hoopes coached students through the opening movements of Ernő Dohnányi’s Piano Quintet in C-Minor, Op. 1, and Johannes Brahms’ Piano Trio No. 2 in C-Major, Op. 87. I have no experience playing a stringed instrument. Watching these sessions gave me a new understanding about how ensembles should interact and play together. I very much appreciate learning more about bowing and pizzicato technique, preserving ensemble balance, and establishing phrasing consistency among the players.

The Prelude Concerts were very entertaining, even though the performers had only been playing together for a few days. Hannah Tam and Hanna Park on violins, Rang Tae on viola, and Shengyu Meng on cello played Wofgang Amadeus Mozart’s String Quartet in G-Major, K-387, the first of his six quartets dedicated to Franz Josef Haydn. They gave this rather introverted work a lively performance. I really liked their feisty approach to the fugue-like finale, which has a recap that doesn’t start with the main fugal melody. Instead, with great anticipation, it reappears at the very end. These players captured the feeling well.

In the next work on the program, Arno Babadjanian’s Piano Trio in F-sharp Minor, pianist Jialin Yao, violinist Jake Dongyoung Shim, and cellist Miles Livine captured the mournful feel of the opening Largo and were on point when the Allegro espressivo started. They played the tuneful secondary theme well, first on piano. As the opening movement came to a conclusion, the strings screeched. It was very effective.

The Badadjanian Trio was repeated the next day. Just before, Jonathan Mak on piano, Julia Schilz on violin, Andy Park on viola, and Amanda Chi on cello played Robert Schumann’s Piano Quartet in E-flat Major, Op. 47. They played the whole quartet and incorporated the previous day’s coaching on the finale. They also decided not to comply with a strange request Schumann made to surreptitiously retune the cello’s C-string a full step lower to B-flat in the middle of the performance. Even well-rehearsed, professional ensembles struggle with this retuning request.

A high point of this year’s festival is Carte Blanche concerts, where pianist Gilles Vonsattel performs the first three of eight concerts of all 32 of Beethoven’s piano sonatas. This project grew out of the COVID-19 pandemic and was the brainchild of Wu Han. He’s not playing them in the order written; instead he’s combing different sonatas from different periods of Beethoven’s compositional career.

The first concert on Thursday evening started with Beethoven’s very first sonata, in F-minor, Op. 2, no 1. It also included Sonata No. 4 in E-flat Major, Op. 7, Sonata No. 24 in G-Major, Op. 79, and, one of Beethoven’s best piano sonatas, No. 21 in C-Major, Op. 53, Waldstein.  

On the night before the performance, Vonsattel offered the most informative discussion of this music I have ever heard. It included brilliantly played snippets illustrating the points he was making. I know these sonatas intimately, but Vonsattel expanded my appreciation big time. In particular, I have never liked Sonata No. 4, and he provided excellent discussion that expanded my enjoyment.

I was hoping that the brilliant playing on Wednesday would carry over to Thursday, but it didn’t. That said, there were many wonderful moments during Vonsattel’s performance. My high school piano teacher used to rave about the mournful secondary theme of the finale of the first sonata, Op. 2 no.1. I was never good enough to play it, but Vonsattel certainly is. In his hands it sounded great. His fingering in the runs closing the exposition of Op. 7 was breathtaking. He honored all of the repeats, including the development and recap of opening movement of Op. 79. Many moments in his performance of the finale of Waldstein were heavenly, especially in the transition to the last run-through of the Rondo finale. The running octaves in the finale’s coda were very good.

Unfortunately, throughout the performance, he seemed a bit off, especially in the second half. Things seemed very rushed in Op. 79 and Waldstein to the point where the runs were not always well articulated. He played the secondary theme in Waldstein’s opening movement too fast, and the contrast with the main theme was diminished. He was reading the music from a tablet inside the piano, and for a brief moment in the Waldstein Rondo he seemed to lose his place and had to do a quick backtrack. The performance seemed very perfunctory, like he wasn’t all that into it. While still very enjoyable, it was a bit of a let-down.

Friday night’s concert No. 3, which was repeated on Saturday, was entitled From Bach. And what, exactly, was from Bach? The fugue, of course, and the program included fugues from Johann Sebastian Bach, Franz Josef Haydn, and Felix Mendelssohn.

First on the program was Bach himself. French pianist Jean-Efflam Bavouzet performed three Prelude and Fugues from Book I of the Well-Tempered Clavier. He gave spirited performances of Fugue No. 7 in E-flat Major, Fugue No. 12 in F-Minor, and Fugue No. 21 in B-Flat Major. In listening to Bach’s keyboard music on a modern fortepiano, it is impossible not to enjoy the benefits of being able to alter dynamically music originally written for a harpsichord, the loudness of which cannot be changed during a performance. Bavouzet played it perfectly.

On the day before, Wu Han had a conversation with Bavouzet about growing up and studying music in France in the 1960s and 1970s. They also discussed Bach’s desire to use these Preludes and Fugues in all 24 major and minor keys to demonstrate the benefits of so-called “well-tempered” tuning, which allows music of any key to be performed on a single keyboard. This reflects the unfortunate reality that there is no such thing as perfect tuning, which is physically impossible. “Well-tempered” tuning, however imperfect, is the best way to overcome this challenge.

Up next was the Viano Quartet performing Haydn’s String Quartet in C-Major, Op. 20, no. 2. Haydn is rightly considered the father of the string quartet, and the notes and discussion focused on how the so-called Sun Quartets from 1772 really set the bar on excellence in string quartet writing. While forward looking, this quartet is one of the three quartets from Op. 20 that ends in a fugue, which had not been in vogue for a generation.

The Viano Quartet of Lucy Wang and Hao Zhou on violin, Aiden Kane on viola, and Tate Zawadiuk on cello gave a wonderful performance of the C-Major quartet. From the beginning of the Moderato, this quartet shows off one of Haydn’s innovations, a balance of all four instruments, starting with Zawadiuk’s dainty and warm interpretation of the opening melody on the cello. They clearly enjoyed playing the fun finale, a fugue in four parts. It was an excellent performance.

The Viano Quartet stayed onstage for the next fugue on the program, Felix Mendelssohn’s Fugue in E-Flat Major, Op 81, no.4. While published late with a high opus number, this charming, slow fugue was completed around the time of my favorite work by Mendelssohn, the String Quartet in A-Minor, Op. 13. The Viano Quartet played it very delicately, with wonderful interactions. It was a nice way to end the concert’s first half.

Bach inspired the first work in the second half, Fancy on a Bach Air for solo cello, written by John Corigliano in 1996. After explaining that he had played it at the very first Music@Menlo festival in 2002, David Finckel gave a heartfelt rendition. Over the years, he has always produced the warmest and cleanest of tones on the cello, and he did so again on Friday night.

The evening ended with my single favorite piece of music by Johannes Brahms, String Sextet No. 2 in G-Major, Op. 36. There are many things about this work that I love, but the most significant is the ending of the Adagio third movement, an unusual set of variations on a rather oblique and mysterious theme. Following a couple of robust and dramatic variations, it ends with the most beautiful and angelic music Brahms ever wrote.

When hearing a favorite piece, I always have great anticipation, but there’s also trepidation. Will it be any good? The assembled musicians offered great promise. Chad Hoopes and Guillermo Figueroa, a last-minute substitution, on violins, Paul Neubauer and Matthew Lipman on violas, and David Finckel and Jonathan Swensen on cellos.

Unfortunately, on Friday night they were hampered by the bugaboo of a large, chamber ensemble of strings: intonation, which, on this occasion, was not “well-tempered.” From the tremolos that open the Allegro non troppo through the crescendo to the climactic ending of the opening themes, the playing was great, but it just didn’t resonate consistently. Sometimes it was outright coarse. There were several moments of brilliance, like when the cello and viola came in for the lovely secondary theme, and the famous a-g-a-th-e that closes the exposition. On the whole, however, the opening movement was disappointing.

Hoopes tuned up before the Scherzo: Allegro non troppo second movement, and having the first violin in tune improved things considerably. They were having a great time during the frolicking, Presto trio. They all tuned up before my favorite third movement, and from there it was ringingly beautiful. I could not have asked for a more wonderful performance of the angelic Brahms. The finale was resounding. Although the Brahms didn’t start well, it ended great.
