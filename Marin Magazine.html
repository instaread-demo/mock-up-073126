Harbor Porpoises Return to San Francisco Bay: Where to Spot Them in Marin

by Grace Towle and Julie Mirocha 3 days ago
Listen to audio version of this article
Sponsored by
0:00 / 1:46
Instaread

It’s a shark, it’s a dolphin… no wait, it’s a harbor porpoise. After more than 80 years, the once common harbor porpoise has returned. It’s believed that the use of submarine nets in the bay during WWII and the increase in industrial environmental contaminants caused them to relocate, starting in the early 1940s. Today they can be spotted all over the Bay, to the delight of spectators both on and off the water.

Fourth-generation ferry captain Maggie McDonogh, co-owner of the Angel Island Tiburon Ferry, is thrilled. “Growing up, my father would tell me about all the porpoise he would see. He was born on Main Street in Tiburon and spent his life navigating the bay and delta,” McDonogh recalls. “I didn’t see them until approximately 20 years ago.”

According to Bill Keener, The Marine Mammal Center’s go-to porpoise expert, Harbor Porpoises are in the Bay year-round and keep to a loose schedule based on the tides, but their presence also depends on the mix of currents and prey fish. Discernable from other cetaceans found in the area, like the Pacific White-sided Dolphin or Common Bottlenose Dolphin, Harbor Porpoises are dark gray with a white belly — between five and six feet long — and instead of a “bottlenose,” feature a short round face. From a distance their dorsal fin can look like a Hershey’s Kiss. 

The reappearance of Harbor Porpoise in the Bay is a reason to celebrate successes in water quality restoration from the Clean Air and Water Acts of the 1960s and 70s. While they still face threats in other parts of the world from fishing nets and pollution, for now the almost 300 harbor porpoises who make the Bay their home are living out a happy ending. 

Best places to spot Harbor Porpoises in Marin — from Bill Keener and Captain Maggie:

  1. The Golden Gate Bridge’s pedestrian walkway. Walking slowly all the way across and taking time to scan below frequently, will almost always result in a few sightings at high tide. 
  1. Cavallo Point, just south of the Bay Area Discovery Museum, is a good place to observe porpoises — they may get to within 50 feet of the rocks. 
  1. If you’re on a boat, look for them in Racoon Strait and Belvedere Point. 

Pro Tips: High tide is best (check an online tide table to time your visit). Sometimes you will notice gulls hovering above an actively feeding porpoise, hoping to steal some scraps. Bring binoculars with you.
